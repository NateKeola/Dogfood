# The New American Family Physician (1899) | Historical reference

**Scope:** Book identified from the [original reel by @different_script](https://www.instagram.com/reels/Df7v-eHxt3P/). A full, readable scan of the exact edition was not located. This is an edition-level overview and a guide to the archival images, not a page-by-page review, transcript, or set of medical instructions.

**Looking for the actual frames?** See the [exact reel screenshots and timestamped page index](reel-df7v-ehxt3p/README.md). The archival plates below are separate background material.

## What the book is

The reel shows the cover of *The New American Family Physician*, with Lyman, Fenger, and Belfield on the cover. [The 1899 revised edition record](https://cabinetforthecurious.com/library/new-american-family-physician-the/) identifies Geo. M. Hill Co., Chicago, and also credits H. Webster Jones. The original U.S. edition is [in the public domain](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/index.html). The missing pages are an access issue, not a copyright issue.

## What it covers

- **How the body works:** Anatomy and physiology for lay readers, with color illustrations and layered diagrams showing where organs sit in relation to one another.
- **Illness and bedside care:** How physicians of the time described causes, symptoms, and treatments, and what they expected relatives to do for a sick person at home.
- **Hygiene and emergencies:** The book addressed sanitation, prevention and emergency care. This is useful for studying the history of household medicine, not for deciding how to handle an emergency today.
- **Plants and medicines:** The reel highlights one slice of a much broader book. In the related 1898 volume, the botanical plates place familiar culinary plants beside toxic plants. The illustrations document what was studied or used historically, not what is safe to take.
- **What aged poorly:** The [Science History Institute catalogue](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/bg257g476) also identifies phrenology in the 1898 volume. Its presence alongside detailed anatomical illustrations is a reminder that accurate illustrations are not proof that the surrounding medical claims hold up.

## What is available online

- [Exact-title 1899 edition record](https://cabinetforthecurious.com/library/new-american-family-physician-the/): bibliographic details, not a complete scan.
- [Science History Institute: The Book of Health (1898)](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/bg257g476): the institute scanned **19 selected images**, not the full 1,157-page related volume. Its Download menu offers a PDF or high-resolution JPG ZIP of those images. Credit: Courtesy of Science History Institute. The archive marks these images public domain.
- [National Library of Medicine: 20th Century Family Physician (c. 1900)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nlmcatalog/101597196): related 1,157-page revision of *The Book of Health*, with color plates and paper-overlay anatomy charts. The catalog record is not a full-text scan or an exact-edition substitute.

### Botanical plates worth opening first

- [Plate 1](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/hm50ts358): saffron, meadowsweet, hedge mustard, and hedge nettle.
- [Plate 6](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/bv73c1191): garlic and horseradish appear alongside colchicum (meadow saffron). The similar names do not make meadow saffron a substitute for culinary saffron: [Poison Control lists autumn crocus (*Colchicum autumnale*) as poisonous](https://www.poison.org/articles/plant).
- [Plate 12](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/ng451j163): mullein, rosemary, and nettle appear alongside deadly nightshade, which [Poison Control lists as poisonous](https://www.poison.org/articles/plant).
- [Plate 15](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/wp988k57b) and [Plate 16](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/pr76f419j): chamomile and caraway are in the former; dill and peppermint are in the latter. A useful modern comparison: [NCCIH distinguishes peppermint leaf from enteric-coated peppermint oil studied for adult IBS](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/peppermint-oil). Evidence for the latter does not validate peppermint tea, homemade oil, or the book's historical preparations.

**For future notes:** Keep a page or plate reference, the exact edition, the plant's scientific identity if known, and a separate link for current evidence and safety. [NCCIH Herbs at a Glance](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/herbsataglance) is a starting point. Do not use historical recipes or doses for self-treatment.
